The proposed paper offers a new perspective on the problem of AI alignment, inspired by life. 

In the first half of the paper, we highlight a common principle that has been identified separately in many areas of life science and social science: **living systems are healthy and flourishing when they avoid fixation on particular forms**. For example:   
   •  Pursuit of a single drive, such as food or sex, becomes pathological when not balanced with competing drives.   
   •  Monoculture ecosystems are fragile and unhealthy.   
   •  Concentration of power reduces a society's adaptability and resilience.  
     By avoiding collapse, life maintains the potential to continually discover new solutions and patterns of organization. We walk through examples in detail from a range of domains.

The second half of the paper applies this principle to AI alignment. There is broad agreement that aligned AI systems should support futures that are healthy and flourishing. Therefore, **alignment means avoiding fixation on particular forms**. AI and human-AI systems in an aligned future admit *partial* formal descriptions but necessarily defy complete formalization. 

We describe how a large portion of existing AI safety and alignment research can be seen as identifying particular kinds of fixation and developing strategies to mitigate them. For example, in the well-studied problem of reward hacking, AI systems adhere to specified values yet produce bad outcomes because the specification did not capture everything that matters. Reward hacking is fixation to the form of a particular reward function. Many solutions to this problem have been proposed, such as schemes for learning human values dynamically, the use of one AI system to manage another AI system, and methods for mechanistic interpretability to maintain human understanding of AI systems. But in a life-inspired view of alignment, none of these solutions are complete. If we define any particular alignment scheme, and AI grows rapidly in capability with that scheme as its basis, the outcome will be misaligned. 

This life-centric perspective on alignment is useful for two reasons:

1. A great deal is known mechanistically about how living systems avoid fixation. In the first half of the paper, as we work through examples from multiple levels of scale, we identify how healthy living systems rely on *semi-permeable boundaries*. Parts of each system bound and limit other parts so that individual forms are contextualized instead of dominating. For example:  
   •  Cognitive control allows various drives to motivate behavior conditionally, when contextually appropriate.   
   •  Species hold each other in check and create more complex roles for each other in ecosystems.   
   •  Laws and regulations give individuals flexibility to pursue their own motives to a degree while functioning as part of a larger system.   
   Semi-permeable boundaries permit interactions while also supporting the individuated existence of a thriving diversity of forms. 
   
   In the second half of the paper, we translate this mechanistic view to the alignment problem. We outline how an aligned future can be supported by an ever-evolving network of semi-permeable boundaries. We show where this process is currently working well, where there are risks, and what lever points are available.

2. There is a possibility that rapid advances in AI will lead to dramatic changes in the organization of the world. We believe an alignment framework should generalize across changes that could be as substantial as the emergence of nucleotide replicators or multicellular life. Principles of health and flourishing that describe living systems of all kinds, at all levels of scale, are more likely to generalize. 

Finally, we are excited about submitting this article to PNAS. It is interdisciplinary, drawing on theory and data from many fields. The specialities of the authors span neuroscience, AI research, social science, philosophy, physiology and psychiatry. The simple premise of our article is important and previously unarticulated, and it is sure to draw strong opinions from multiple angles. We hope our message will connect with AI researchers; biologists with insights about how their field informs AI safety; social scientists with an interest in AI policy; philosophers who will contrast our ideas with existing moral frameworks.

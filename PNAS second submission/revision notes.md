### Explaining why random noise and capriciousness are fixations

Readers steeped in standard "order vs. chaos" or "explore vs. exploit" framing might misunderstand "fixation" as meaning only static rigidity, and get confused when we include random noise (line 115) and discarding schemes capriciously (line 383) as examples of fixation. We should unpack this in a couple of sentences: literally *anything* that can be captured by a single closed concept or fixed abstraction—including a stationary random distribution (which is a fixed statistical description) or a blanket habit of capriciously discarding commitments—becomes a fixation if given unconditional traction. A crystal and white noise are the same failure mode: both collapse the system onto a simple, unchanging description rather than accumulating context-sensitive depth.

On p. 21, Kashdan and Rottenberg (2010) ask whether flexibility can become instability or impulsiveness. **This is not the kind of flexibility we're talking about**. In our language, instability or impulsiveness is fixation. 

### Restoring the evolutionary bottleneck line in "Healthy for whom?"

In the `Healthy for whom?` section, we currently have this sentence commented out (line 491 of `PNAS second submission.tex`):

> "An AI collective overwriting this structure with its own de novo designs, arising from a tiny bottleneck in evolutionary history, could be a dramatic fixation."

We should bring this sentence back in. It makes the argument against AI replacing existing life follow directly from the core thesis of the paper (that routing the future through a narrow bottleneck is a dramatic fixation), rather than sounding like a general appeal to preserving heritage.

### Why getting quick answers from AI could slow us down in the long run

When people worked on problems somewhat independently in parallel, they kept coming up with divergent innovative solutions. If everyone can quickly get an answer from AI, they might be less motivated to do that. 

## 18 November 2026

``what works locally can undermine the conditions it depends on"

(1) What do we think about selection vs other kinds of pressures? I'm thinking of examples like humans setting up antitrust laws. In the classic MLS setup, you have many societies, and the ones that create antitrust laws outcompete the ones that don't. But it's also possible that you have just one society and the people deduce that it would be a good idea to have antitrust laws. Then the 'outer pressure' isn't really selection anymore. 
(2) What do we think about normal 'group' selection versus what you could call 'peer-level' effects? In the MLS view of multicellular organisms, for example, the outer pressure is selection at the organism level. On the other hand, in ecosystems, each creature is selfishly selected for being a barrier on the extremities of the other creatures, which looks more like a peer-level effect. (Although you could also take a gaia-like view and say that group selection for entire ecosystems creates ones that have good internal barriers.) Similarly, in a game of chess, one player's individual selfish motives place a barrier on the other player, to avoid a collapsed lopsided game. It's not a group-level effect but a peer-level constraint.

If I fill in these two questions with my own current best understanding, the general answer looks something like: **there's an effect from the context an entity is in, which places a limit on it**. But it (1) doesn't have to be selection, and (2) doesn't have to be at the group level.

Also see papers like this:
https://timwaring.info/wp-content/uploads/2012/03/sloanwilsonbbs-full.pdf
which talk about how 'reasoning' is like an extension of evolution. Related to the evolution of evolvability. This could bridge between classic multi-level selection and our framework. Evolution of the evolvability says that the outer pressure of evolution creates the inner optimization of reasoning. And then you have the generalization of MLS where the outer pressure is that evolved reasoning.

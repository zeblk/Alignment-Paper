Dear PNAS Editors,

We submit our manuscript, \textit{Living Alignment}, for consideration as a Perspective in PNAS. Our paper offers a new perspective on the problem of AI alignment, inspired by life. A unique feature of this paper is that we perform an extensive synthesis of literature from across the life and social sciences. It turns out these sciences have already discovered an essential principle that speaks directly to the alignment problem.

In the first half of the paper, we identify a common principle that has been discovered separately in multiple disciplines: \textbf{living systems are healthy and flourishing when they avoid fixation on particular forms}. By avoiding fixation, life maintains the potential to continually invent new solutions and patterns of organization. We walk through examples in detail from a range of domains, including evolutionary biology, cell biology, neuroscience, cognitive science, social science and ecology. 

The second half of the paper applies the same principle to AI alignment. There is broad agreement that aligned AI systems should support futures that are healthy and flourishing. Therefore, we reason that \textbf{alignment means avoiding fixation on particular forms}. AI, human-AI and broader ecological systems in an aligned future admit \emph{partial} formal descriptions but necessarily defy complete formalization. We use the problem of reward hacking to illustrate the message in detail, then discuss more generally what negative and positive outcomes look like in the living alignment framework.

If AI drives large changes in the world, this level of abstraction---where the science of life teaches us what it means for any kind of system to be healthy and flourishing---may generalize better to new patterns of organization. 

We are excited about submitting this article to PNAS. The article integrates theory and data from multiple fields and draws heavily on work published in PNAS. Our specialities span neuroscience, cognitive science, AI research, social science, physiology and psychiatry. The simple premise of our article is important and previously unarticulated, and it is sure to engage debate.
